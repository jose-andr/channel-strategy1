# 2. Research context

The research was undertaken to understand why the Rates team had comparatively strong customer satisfaction and to identify further opportunities to improve the customer and employee experience as Melbourne grows.

The report notes:

- approximately 189,000 residents and 110,000 homes in 2025
- forecast growth to approximately 292,000 residents and 230,000 homes by 2050
- Rates CSAT of approximately 85%
- approximately 8,500 Salesforce enquiries per year

The research question was:

> How might we understand customer and employee experience so that we can improve the rates experience?

---

# 3. Research approach

The research combined qualitative and quantitative inputs.

### 3.1.1. Qualitative research

Interviews included:

- 5 current City of Melbourne ratepayers
- 5 future ratepayers
- 5 City of Melbourne employees

### 3.1.2. Supporting evidence

The research also drew on:

- two-week Rates data capture
- Genesys call evidence
- customer interaction records
- Google Analytics
- previous City of Melbourne CX work
- industry research

The report explicitly blends these sources to form the customer insights.

### 3.1.3. Evidence caveat

The qualitative sample is intentionally small.

The interview findings should therefore be treated as **Rates-specific evidence and design signals**, strengthened where they align with larger interaction and analytics datasets.

They should not automatically be generalised across all City of Melbourne services.

# 4. Customer mix

The March 2025 data capture identified a customer mix including:

- Ratepayers — 42.3%
- Real Estate Agents — 17.1%
- Solicitors / Conveyancers — 15.1%
- Other — 10.1%
- Landata / Dye & Durham — 8.1%

This is important because Rates demand is not generated only by residential ratepayers.

Professional intermediaries and property-related organisations are also material users of the service.

---

# 5. Behavioural themes

The research identified four cross-cutting themes:

## 5.1. Trust

Customers expect:

- reliable information
- trusted online and offline sources
- consistent information
- credible explanations

Access to reliable information was identified as an important contributor to customer trust.

## 5.2. Safety

Customers discussed safety at both physical and digital levels.

For digital services, safety includes confidence that:

- personal information is protected
- account access is secure
- transactions are legitimate
- authentication is appropriate

## 5.3. Control

Customers expect to be able to:

- access their information
- manage personal details
- make changes
- manage payments
- control common account interactions

These expectations are influenced by customers' experience with banks, utilities and other government services.

## 5.4. Value

Customers want to understand:

- what rates pay for
- why rates cost what they do
- how the city or community benefits
- what value their contribution creates

The research found that different customers define value differently, but the need for transparency was common.

# 6. Six customer insights

The report organises the research into six customer needs.

## 6.1. Insight 1 — Help me understand my rates

Customers need clearer information about:

- what rates pay for
- how rates are calculated
- how valuations affect rates
- why an amount may change

### 6.1.1. Evidence

The research found:

- 7 of 10 customers were not confident explaining what rates pay for or how rates are calculated
- customers typically research first
- customers move to phone or email when they cannot find sufficient information online
- 17,800 active users visited the Rates website section between January and May 2025
- 1,597 searches were recorded for "How rates are calculated"

The report identifies a lack of clear summary and detailed rates information as a source of additional contact.

### 6.1.2. Intelligent Front Door relevance

This supports:

- intent-led information
- clearer information hierarchy
- simple and detailed explanation layers
- stronger off-platform search treatment
- direct Routine information fulfilment

---

## 6.2. Insight 2 — Provide me with a consistent experience

Customers expect the service to work consistently across:

- mobile
- desktop
- digital support
- phone
- offline channels

Customers may use different devices depending on how much information they need.

The research identifies an expectation that City of Melbourne should provide a digital experience comparable with other organisations customers regularly use.

The report recommends a digital-first trusted experience with access to support, rather than isolated channel experiences.

### 6.2.1. Intelligent Front Door relevance

This reinforces the Connected Interactions principle:

**customers may change channel, but the service logic should remain coherent.**

Define should consider consistency in:

- terminology
- authoritative information
- next actions
- support availability
- account context

---

## 6.3. Insight 3 — Help me feel safe and secure

Customers expect secure digital access when managing personal and financial information.

The research identified expectations for:

- account login
- password-based access
- two-step verification
- familiar authentication patterns

The report notes that 14 of 15 interview participants discussed digital experiences involving login and password.

### 6.3.1. Intelligent Front Door relevance

Authentication should be treated as part of interaction design.

The level of authentication should be proportionate to the task.

Not every front-door interaction needs authentication, but account and personal-data interactions may require stronger trust controls.

---

## 6.4. Insight 4 — Help me feel in control

Customers expect to be able to manage common Rates account activities themselves.

The research specifically identifies expectations around:

- payments
- direct debit
- payment preferences
- BPAY
- annual versus instalment payment
- account details

Employee research also indicated that self-service for payment arrangements and direct debit could reduce manual effort.

The report estimates a combined **10,478 payment and direct debit enquiries**, illustrating material Routine demand associated with these tasks.

### 6.4.1. Intelligent Front Door relevance

This is strong evidence for:

**Routine — Transact / Track**

with self-service where safe, while retaining Guided or Assisted support where the customer's circumstances require it.

---

## 6.5. Insight 5 — Provide me with access and support

The research does not support a digital-only service model.

The Rates team has one of the stronger CSAT results in the organisation, and the report explores the role of staff support in that experience.

The data capture found a large proportion of Rates enquiries were handled through email, alongside phone and Salesforce-managed pathways.

The research also estimates significant labour cost associated with staff completing basic activities customers may be capable of self-serving.

### 6.5.1. Intelligent Front Door relevance

The objective should be:

**make Routine tasks easier to complete independently**

while still allowing customers to access:

- Guided support
- specialist expertise
- non-digital support
- assistance where the digital journey fails

Better self-service and better assisted support are complementary.

---

## 6.6. Insight 6 — Help me understand how I contribute to my city

Customers want greater transparency about:

- where rates revenue goes
- what priorities are funded
- why those priorities are chosen
- what value is created for the community

The report identifies a gap between perceptions of high rates and understanding the services City of Melbourne provides.

It suggests this gap can itself generate enquiries.

### 6.6.1. Intelligent Front Door relevance

For Rates, authoritative information should address both:

**what do I need to do?**

and, where relevant:

**why is this happening?**

This is primarily a Rates-specific information and trust requirement.

# 9. Implications for Intelligent Front Door

The Rates research supports several broader Intelligent Front Door design signals.

## 9.1. Start from customer intent

Customers frequently begin with:

- Google
- a question
- a payment need
- an account need

rather than an internal service label.

---

## 9.2. Separate information from interpretation

Routine information should be easy to access.

Specialist interpretation should remain available for:

- valuations
- individual calculations
- disputed or unusual circumstances

---

## 9.3. Make Routine account activity easier

Customers expect common account actions to be manageable without staff assistance.

This supports direct fulfilment for appropriate Routine interactions.

---

## 9.4. Design for movement between channels

Customer behaviour can look like:

**mobile search**  
→ **desktop research**  
→ **self-service**  
→ **human support**

The objective is not to force a single channel.

The objective is to preserve a coherent interaction.

---

## 9.5. Preserve support access

Digital-first should not mean digital-only.

Support needs vary by:

- task
- confidence
- accessibility
- customer circumstances
- complexity
- risk

---

## 9.6. Treat security as part of the experience

Customers evaluate digital services partly through whether the experience feels safe and trustworthy.

Authentication should therefore be designed proportionately to the interaction.

# 10. Relevance to support modes

The Rates research reinforces the task-level interaction-mode model.

### 10.1.1. Routine

Examples:

- retrieve rates information
- view balance
- confirm payment
- manage standard account activity

Typical need:

**Inform / Transact / Track**

### 10.1.2. Guided

Examples:

- navigate account changes
- understand which process applies
- complete a task where additional help is required

Typical need:

**Identify / Navigate / Complete with help**

### 10.1.3. Assisted

Examples:

- valuation explanation
- complex rates calculation
- hardship or sensitive circumstances
- exception handling

Typical need:

**Resolve / Specialist / Sensitive**

The service itself should not be assigned one interaction mode.

The mode depends on the specific task and customer circumstances.

# 11. Relevance to Define

The Rates research supports carrying the following questions into Define:

1. How might customers reach authoritative Rates information more easily from search?
2. What Routine Rates tasks should be directly self-serviceable?
3. What minimum confirmation and status information should customers receive?
4. What account context needs to persist when customers move into assisted support?
5. Which interactions require Guided versus specialist support?
6. What level of authentication is appropriate for different account tasks?
7. How should support remain accessible when customers cannot or do not complete a digital pathway?
8. Which Rates patterns are reusable across other services, and which are service-specific?
