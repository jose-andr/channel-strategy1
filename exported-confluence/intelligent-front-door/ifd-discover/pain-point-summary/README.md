## 1.1. Purpose

Identify where customers experience avoidable effort, confusion, failed pathways or unnecessary channel movement across priority Intelligent Front Door interactions.

This artefact brings together evidence from:

- priority service analysis
- contact reason and task analysis
- current-state interaction flows
- routing and transfer behaviour
- website and form gap analysis
- internal site-search behaviour
- known self-service friction
- repeat / chase contact signals
- Rates customer and employee research

The purpose is to identify **problems that should move into Define**.

It does not prescribe the future-state solution or complete root-cause analysis for every issue.

# 4. Headline discovery finding

The evidence increasingly suggests that customers are already trying to use digital channels.

The recurring problem is often not:

**Will customers adopt digital?**

It is:

**Can customers successfully find, understand and complete the right task?**

The current pattern is often:

**Find**  
→ **Understand**  
→ **Complete**  
→ **Confirm**  
→ **Get help when needed**  
→ **Preserve context if the interaction moves**

Customer effort increases when one of these steps breaks.

# 5. Pain point themes

## 5.1. Customers cannot always find the right service or pathway

**Theme:** Findability and routing  
**Priority:** High  
**Confidence:** High  
**Evidence status:** Evidence-based

### 5.1.1. Evidence

CFT-2712 identifies significant demand falling into General Enquiries or remaining difficult to attribute.

CFT-2713 shows General Enquiry carrying mixed customer needs across multiple services.

The Channel Strategy data analysis adds stronger website evidence:

- **77% of identified website issues** relate to customers being unable to find what they need or not understanding the process
- internal-search demand is heavily concentrated around high-volume services such as Parking Permits, Parking Information, Parking Infringements and Rates
- customers often move into assisted channels when the required pathway is not visible or understandable

The evidence does **not** support treating this as purely a content problem or purely a findability problem.

The combined evidence points to issues across:

- search
- navigation
- service naming
- process clarity
- pathway visibility
- form access
- missing or broken journeys

### 5.1.2. Customer effect

Customers may:

- not know which service applies
- search across multiple pages
- enter through General Enquiries
- reach the wrong team first
- be redirected
- abandon digital self-service
- contact staff simply to find the correct pathway

### 5.1.3. Operational effect

Staff may need to:

- interpret broad customer language
- identify the relevant service
- provide navigation help
- redirect or transfer customers
- answer information available elsewhere

### 5.1.4. Define implication

Explore how the front door can recognise:

**customer intent**  
→ **task**  
→ **appropriate pathway**

without requiring customers to understand City of Melbourne's internal service structure.

## 5.2. General Enquiries is acting as a navigation layer

**Theme:** Front-door discovery failure  
**Priority:** High  
**Confidence:** High  
**Evidence status:** Evidence-based

### 5.2.1. Evidence

The Channel Strategy data analysis reports that approximately **55% of General Enquiries calls are information-seeking only**.

The analysis describes General Enquiries as functioning partly as a navigation and search support service.

Customer needs represented in this space include:

- how to contact the correct service
- where to send an enquiry
- who handles a permit or application
- whether a form or enquiry was received
- how to reach a specific team
- where to find information

### 5.2.2. Customer effect

Customers may need a person simply to discover:

- which service applies
- where to go
- how to make contact
- whether an earlier interaction was received

### 5.2.3. Operational effect

General-support capacity is used for:

- service discovery
- navigation
- information retrieval
- manual routing

### 5.2.4. Define implication

Treat General Enquiries as a **diagnostic signal of front-door failure**, not simply as a service category.

The opportunity is to resolve more intent before the customer reaches General Enquiries.

## 5.3. Information needs are generating avoidable assisted contact

**Theme:** Avoidable information contact  
**Priority:** High  
**Confidence:** High  
**Evidence status:** Evidence-based

### 5.3.1. Evidence

CFT-2712 identifies a significant information-seeking component within General Enquiry demand.

CFT-2713 identifies high-volume Routine information topics including:

- parking finance enquiries
- parking prosecution enquiries
- construction information
- rates notices
- balance and payment confirmation

Rates + CXU found that customers generally prefer to research and self-serve first, then contact City of Melbourne when available information does not answer their question.

The Rates research found:

- 7 of 10 interviewed customers were not confident explaining what rates pay for or how rates are calculated
- 17,800 active users visited the Rates website section between January and May 2025
- 1,597 searches were recorded for "How rates are calculated"

The Channel Strategy analysis reinforces that many high-volume contacts are simple questions about:

- where to find a form
- what evidence is needed
- how to pay
- how to update something
- what happens next

### 5.3.2. Customer effect

Customers may need to contact staff for information that could potentially be:

- easier to locate
- easier to understand
- better structured
- more task-specific
- surfaced earlier

### 5.3.3. Operational effect

Staff capacity is used for information retrieval and explanation rather than interactions requiring judgement.

### 5.3.4. Define implication

Separate:

**Routine — Inform**

from:

**Guided / Assisted interpretation**

Improving Routine information should reduce avoidable contact without attempting to automate specialist advice.

## 5.7. Many high-volume questions are simple even when the service is complex

**Theme:** Question complexity versus service complexity  
**Priority:** High  
**Confidence:** High  
**Evidence status:** Evidence-based

### 5.7.1. Evidence

The Channel Strategy analysis identifies common questions such as:

- How do I update my parking permit?
- How do I pay my rates?
- Where do I find the form?
- Why won't the website let me do this?

However, CFT-2713 shows that the underlying service can still be Routine, Guided or Assisted.

For example:

- Construction Permit → Guided

### 5.7.3. Define implication

Avoid designing the entire support pathway around the complexity of the internal service.

# 8. Pain points to move into Define

## 8.1. Intent and pathway recognition

**Problem**

Customers cannot always identify the right service or pathway.

**Define question**

How might we help customers reach the correct task without requiring them to understand internal service structures?

---

## 8.2. Routine information and task completion

**Problem**

Simple, predictable interactions can generate assisted demand because information, navigation or self-service is insufficient.

**Define question**

How might we make Routine information, transactions, tracking and reporting easier to complete?

---

## 8.3. Guided decision support

**Problem**

Customers sometimes need help with eligibility, evidence, pathway choice or process understanding.

**Define question**

How might we provide enough guidance for customers to reach the correct next step without unnecessary human triage?

---

## 8.4. Failed self-service recovery

**Problem**

Customers can move from digital into assisted channels when a pathway fails and may need to restart.

**Define question**

How might customers recover from failed self-service without repeating information or starting again?

---

## 8.5. Status and next action

**Problem**

Customers may contact City of Melbourne simply to understand whether an action was received or what happens next.

**Define question**

How might customers receive clear confirmation, status, timing and next-action information?

---

## 8.6. Context-preserving handoff

**Problem**

Necessary movement between channels and teams can require customers and staff to reconstruct context.

**Define question**

What minimum information should move with an interaction to support continuity?

---

## 8.7. Assisted and exception recognition

**Problem**

Urgent, specialist, sensitive or exceptional needs may initially resemble standard customer tasks.

**Define question**

How might the front door recognise when an interaction requires a higher level of support?

---

## 8.8. Account control and Routine self-service

**Problem**

Customers expect to manage common account and payment activities themselves, but some current services still require staff intervention.

**Define question**

Which Routine account activities can safely be self-served while retaining support where needed?

---

## 8.9. Connected experience standards

**Problem**

Customers may move between devices, digital channels and assisted channels during one interaction.

**Define question**

What minimum standards should remain consistent as an interaction moves between channels?

---

## 8.10. Trust and secure access

**Problem**

Customers expect account interactions to use familiar and proportionate security controls.

**Define question**

What authentication level is appropriate for each interaction type?

---

## 8.11. Inclusive access

**Problem**

Standard digital pathways may not meet every customer's accessibility, language or non-digital support needs.

**Define question**

How might accessible and assisted options remain part of the core interaction model?

# 9. Discovery signal — fix fundamentals before adding orchestration

The Channel Strategy analysis suggests that some high-volume customer effort may be reduced by improving existing service fundamentals before introducing more complex orchestration.

Priority foundations include:

- search
- navigation
- findability
- pathway clarity
- authoritative information
- form completion
- broken links and journeys
- confirmation and status

This should be tested in Define before assuming that new routing, AI or automation is required.

The implication is not:

**do not use AI**

It is:

**Do not use orchestration technology to compensate for unresolved service fundamentals.**

Potential future capabilities such as:

- intent recognition
- AI-supported routing
- automation
- conversational guidance

should be applied where they solve a validated customer or operational problem.

# 10. Cross-cutting discovery findings

## 10.1. The problem is not simply channel adoption

The current evidence suggests many customers already prefer to begin digitally.

The challenge is often:

**channel completion**

rather than:

**channel adoption**

---

## 10.2. Search is part of the service journey

Search should not be treated only as a pre-journey activity.

For many customers the interaction begins with:

**Google / site search**  
→ **information**  
→ **task pathway**

Failure at search or navigation can generate General Enquiry, phone or email demand.

---

## 10.3. Customer behaviour spans channels

A customer may:

**search on mobile**  
→ **research on desktop**  
→ **self-serve**  
→ **seek human support**

The objective is not to select a single "best" channel.

It is to support a coherent interaction.

---

## 10.4. Routine does not mean digital-only

Routine describes the support need.

A Routine task may still require Guided or Assisted support because of:

- accessibility needs
- language needs
- digital failure
- vulnerability
- uncertainty
- exception

---

## 10.5. Better self-service and better support are complementary

Discovery supports both:

- reducing avoidable assisted demand for simple tasks
- improving the quality of human support when it is genuinely needed

These are not competing objectives.
