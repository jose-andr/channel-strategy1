## Purpose of this phase

The Define phase turns Information Architecture discovery evidence into a clear problem frame, agreed scope, success measures and priority opportunities.

The goal is to make sure the team understands the cross-channel IA problem before moving into Design.

This phase should answer:

**Do we have the right IA problem, scope, naming principles and success measures to move forward into Design?**

---

## Define question

**What information architecture problem are we solving, for whom, and what needs to be true for customers to find, understand and use the right service pathway across channels?**

---

## What “ready to move forward” means

Define is complete enough to move into Design when we can answer the following questions with reasonable confidence.

| Readiness question | What good looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Do we have a clear IA problem statement? | The problem is written in plain English and supported by discovery evidence |
| Do we know who the IA work is for? | Customer, staff, channel owner and service owner needs are clearly identified |
| Do we know which journeys and channels are in scope? | Scope is specific enough to guide naming, grouping, routing and channel offer decisions |
| Do we know which IA issues matter most? | Priority opportunities are selected using evidence, demand, customer impact and feasibility |
| Do we know what success looks like? | Measures are defined, even if baselines are incomplete |
| Do we know the current-state versus future-state boundary? | Immediate fixes are separated from changes needing deeper design, governance or technology work |
| Do we know the key constraints? | Policy, legal, accessibility, data, reporting, technology and ownership constraints are visible |
| Do we know what decisions are needed? | Open decisions are documented with owners |
| Do we have enough to start designing? | The team can move into future-state IA patterns, naming standards, channel offer models and routing improvements |

## IA principles

These principles should guide the next stage of work.

| Principle | What it means for Information Architecture |
| --- | --- |
| Customer language first | Labels should reflect how customers describe their task or need, not only internal terminology |
| One service, recognisable names | Priority services should be recognisable across website, Contact Us, IVR, Digital Assistant and staff channels |
| Task clarity | Customers should know what action they can take, such as pay, apply, report, request, check status or get help |
| Channel clarity | Customers should understand what each channel can and cannot do |
| Assisted pathways are visible | Customers who cannot self-serve should know how to get help |
| Current-state before future-state | Immediate fixes should be separated from larger IA redesign or technology-dependent changes |
| Map labels to data structures | Customer-facing labels should be mapped to internal service codes, categories or reporting structures |
| Reuse before reinvention | IA patterns should be reusable across services and channels |
| Accessible and inclusive by design | Naming, grouping and navigation should support disability, low literacy, language and non-digital needs |
| Governed and maintainable | IA changes should have clear ownership so inconsistency does not return |

## Current-state versus future-state framing

Use this section to confirm how opportunities will be classified before Design.

| Category | Meaning | Example | Design implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Now | Can likely be improved within current channels, ownership and governance | Rename a confusing label or update a Contact Us category | Move into quick design or delivery planning |
| Later | Valuable but needs more design, validation or sequencing | Redesign grouping for a complex service area | Move into Design as future-state pattern |
| Dependent | Requires another decision, system change, governance change or service owner agreement | Change IVR routing or reporting taxonomy | Capture dependency and decision pathway |
| Do not progress | Not enough evidence, too low value or outside scope | Whole-of-site restructure unrelated to priority journeys | Record rationale and park |

## Channel offer definition

Use this section to define the channel offer categories that should guide Design.

| Channel offer | Definition | Design implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Information | Customer can read or understand service information | Ensure clear page title, summary and next step |
| Transaction | Customer can complete a payment, application, report or request | Make the task pathway visible and action-oriented |
| Status | Customer can check progress or receive an update | Clarify where and how status can be checked |
| Guidance | Customer can work out what option applies to them | Provide decision support, eligibility guidance or routing prompts |
| Assisted support | Customer can get help from staff | Make support options visible and accessible |
| Escalation | Customer can move to specialist support | Define handoff conditions and specialist pathway |
| Not available | Channel does not currently support this need | Avoid implying the customer can complete the task there |

## Constraints

| Constraint | Impact | Design implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Policy / regulatory | Some service names or terms may have formal meanings | Use plain English labels with formal terms where required |
| Privacy / consent | Some pathways may involve personal or sensitive information | Make escalation and secure support pathways clear |
| Records | IA decisions and changes may need to be documented for traceability | Record decisions and maintain label mapping |
| Accessibility | Labels and navigation must be understandable and usable for diverse customers | Use plain English, accessibility review and assisted pathway visibility |
| Technology | Some channels may limit labels, menu depth, routing or metadata | Design channel-specific variants within shared IA principles |
| Data quality | Current categories may not align across channels | Map labels to service codes and document data gaps |
| Reporting continuity | Changing labels may affect trend reporting | Maintain mapping between old and new terms |
| Operational capacity | Channel owners may have limited capacity to update and maintain labels | Prioritise high-impact fixes and define ownership |
| Governance | Without ongoing ownership, naming inconsistency may return | Design minimum viable IA governance |

## Define exit criteria

Define can move into Design when:

- the IA problem statement is clear and evidence-based
- priority customer, staff, channel owner and service owner needs are agreed
- in-scope and out-of-scope journeys and channels are documented
- priority IA opportunities are selected
- success measures are defined
- IA principles are agreed enough to guide design
- current-state fixes are separated from future-state or dependent opportunities
- channel offer categories are defined
- key label and taxonomy decisions are documented
- reporting and data impacts are visible
- accessibility and plain language considerations are included
- open decisions have owners
- assumptions are documented
- there is enough clarity to create future-state IA patterns, naming standards, channel offer models and routing improvements

## Final Define outcome

By the end of Define, the team should have a clear and agreed view of the Information Architecture problem, scope, outcomes, principles and priority opportunities.

Most importantly, we should be able to answer:

**Do we have the right IA problem, scope, naming principles and success measures to move forward into Design?**

If the answer is yes, the work can progress into future-state IA patterns, naming standards, channel offer models and routing improvements.

If the answer is partial or no, the team should identify the specific gaps, decisions or evidence needed before moving forward.
